For Dental Practices

How much should a dental practice spend on marketing?

Established practices generally run 4-7% of gross revenue. New practices in their first year or two commonly spend far more — published guidance ranges from 6-10% up to 15-20% — because empty chairs cost more than marketing does. But the percentage matters less than what a patient costs you and what a patient is worth.

The benchmarks

What practices actually spend

The published ranges cluster tightly for established practices and diverge sharply for new ones.

▸Established practices: 4-7% The consistent benchmark across healthcare marketing sources. For a practice collecting $1 million annually that is roughly $40,000-$70,000 a year.
▸Maintenance mode: 3-4% Stable volume with no growth ambition can sustain patient flow at the low end.
▸New practices: 6-20% Guidance varies widely, but the direction is consistent — front-load the first 12-24 months. The cost of under-investing at this stage is measured in years of slower growth.
▸Typical monthly spend: $2,000-$8,000 With larger multi-provider practices commonly running $8,000-$15,000 or more.
The better number

Cost per patient, against patient value

This is where the percentage stops being useful and the arithmetic starts.

01

What a patient costs

Reported acquisition costs run roughly $150-$300 for general dentistry and $400-$800 for implant and cosmetic cases. The variance is the point: high-value cases cost more to acquire and are worth it.

02

What a patient is worth

Average annual revenue per patient multiplied by how long they stay. A patient generating around $650 a year over seven years is worth roughly $4,500 — which reframes a $300 acquisition cost entirely.

03

The conversion gap

Many practices convert only a minority of inbound calls to appointments. Fixing that costs nothing in ad spend and changes every other number on this page.

The blind spot

Referrals and AI both run on trust signals

Referrals account for a substantial share of new patients at established practices, and referred patients convert at far higher rates than advertising-generated ones. The same signals that drive referral now drive AI recommendation.

▸Most patients research online first Recent survey data indicates a large majority of patients search online before scheduling, even when they arrived via referral. The referral gets you considered; the search decides it.
▸Reviews are read almost universally Consumer review surveys consistently find that nearly all consumers read reviews for local businesses, and healthcare providers are among the most reviewed categories.
▸Assistants name two or three practices When a patient asks an assistant for a good implant dentist nearby, it recommends practices whose specialisation it can actually verify from the page. Most practice sites never state it in text.
Answers

Common questions

?What percentage of revenue should a dental practice spend on marketing? Established practices generally spend 4-7% of gross revenue. New practices commonly spend considerably more in their first 12-24 months, with published guidance ranging from 6-10% up to 15-20%, to build the patient base that later generates referrals.
?What does a new dental patient cost? Reported acquisition costs run roughly $150-$300 for general dentistry and $400-$800 for implants and cosmetic cases. Costs vary meaningfully by location and channel, with referrals consistently the cheapest and highest-converting source.
?How do I attract higher-value cases instead of cleanings? Publish genuinely specific content on the procedures you want. Patients considering implants research for weeks and have particular questions about grafting, timeline and cost structure. A generic services page answers none of them, so those patients select a practice that demonstrated depth.
?Is my marketing budget too small? Less often than practices assume. The more common problem is spend without measurement — published analyses repeatedly find practices tracking return by channel achieve better results at lower percentages than practices spending more without tracking.
